= Tutorial 4: A tolerance stack-up
:page-id: tutorial-stackup

[.lead]
Reproduce a worked example from B. R. Fischer's _Mechanical Tolerance Stackup and Analysis_ (2nd ed., Figures 8.9–8.14): a chain of four dimensions, analysed worst case, by root-sum-square, and by Monte Carlo simulation. Then drag a tolerance and watch the reject rate change.

image::stackup-canvas.png[The stack-up definition with its report and Monte Carlo histogram]

Reference: click btn:[Stack-up] on the canvas toolbar to load the finished definition.

== The problem

A gap *A–B* is set by one overall dimension minus three part dimensions. Following the chain from A to B, the overall dimension points towards B (+) and the three parts point back (−):

[cols="1,1,1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Dimension |Nominal |Tolerance |Direction
|A to B (overall) |56.5 |±1.5 |+1
|Part 1 |11 |±1 |−1
|Part 2 |17.5 |±0.5 |−1
|Part 3 |16.5 |±0.5 |−1
|===

What are the smallest and largest gaps, and how many assemblies fall outside a gap specification of 10 to 13?

== Build it

. Place four *Dimension* (Tolerance) components, one per row of the table. On each, use menu:Set value…[] on the inputs:
** *N* (nominal), *U* (upper deviation, for example `1.5`) and *L* (lower deviation, for example `-1.5`);
** *D* (direction): `1` for the overall dimension, `-1` for the three parts;
** *Nm* (name): the label used in the report.
. Place a *Stack-up* (Tolerance). Wire *each Dimension D → Stack-up D*, in chain order, holding kbd:[Shift] when you release so the wires are added rather than replaced.
. Place a *Monte Carlo Stack-up* (Tolerance). Wire the four Dimensions into its *D* the same way (with kbd:[Shift]). Set its *LSL* to `10` and *USL* to `13`.
. Place three *Panels*: wire *Stack-up R → Panel* (the report), *Monte Carlo H → Panel* (the histogram) and *Monte Carlo M*, *σ* and *%* into a third Panel.

To make the overall tolerance adjustable, add a slider _±tol 56.5: 1.5 (0 – 3)_, wire it into the first Dimension's *U*, and wire it through a *Multiplication* with *B* set to `-1` into the same Dimension's *L*. A second slider _RSS factor: 1.5 (1 – 2)_ wired into Stack-up's *F* sets the RSS adjustment factor.

== Read the results

The report Panel shows each dimension with its share of the worst-case total, then the three results:

[cols="2,1,2,3",options="header"]
|===
|Method |Tolerance |Gap limits |Meaning
|Worst case |±3.5 |8.0 … 15.0 |Every tolerance at its limit at once: the gap can never fall outside this.
|RSS |±1.9365 |9.5635 … 13.4365 |√(1.5² + 1² + 0.5² + 0.5²): the probable range if each dimension varies independently.
|Adjusted RSS (× 1.5) |±2.9047 |8.5953 … 14.4047 |RSS with a safety factor for processes that are not perfectly centred.
|===

Fischer's book prints ±1.94 and ±2.91 for the last two: he rounds the RSS to 1.94 before multiplying by 1.5. The exact values are the ones above.

The Monte Carlo panel builds 10,000 random assemblies, treating each tolerance as three standard deviations of a normal distribution. Its standard deviation comes out close to RSS / 3 ≈ 0.645, and about 2 % of the builds fall outside 10 – 13. The histogram shows the familiar bell curve.

== Things to try

* Drag *±tol 56.5* down to 0.5. Worst case drops to ±2.5, and the share of rejects falls sharply.
* Set *RSS factor* to 1: adjusted RSS equals plain RSS.
* Set a dimension's *S* (distribution) input to `uniform` or `triangular`. Worst case and RSS do not change; the Monte Carlo spread does.
* Change the Monte Carlo *Seed* to see how much the reject rate moves from one set of 10,000 builds to another.

See xref:tolerance.adoc[Tolerance analysis] for the formulas and how each method should be read.
